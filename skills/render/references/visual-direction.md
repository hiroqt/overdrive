# Visual Direction and Anti-Slop

Read this for a new surface, substantial redesign, or critique of a generic-looking interface.

## Choose a direction from the product

Start from the intended user, main task, content, brand, and current design system. Decide what must be noticed first and what the user should do next. Describe a small visual thesis in plain language: hierarchy, layout rhythm, type and color character, material or imagery, and a recognizable detail. The thesis should help make implementation decisions; it is not a mood-board paragraph.

Inspect existing routes and shared components before inventing new styles. Extend a sound system. When the request asks for a new visual world, preserve required product behavior and contracts while making the change explicit.

## Watch for defaults

Look for repeated choices that appear to come from a generic template rather than user or product needs:

- every page opens with an eyebrow, large centered headline, short subtitle, and two buttons;
- every content group becomes the same row of rounded cards;
- multiple gradients, glass blur, shadows, and pill badges compete for attention;
- the same font, type weight, spacing, and alignment are applied regardless of content;
- fake ratings, logos, customer quotes, dashboard metrics, or urgency labels decorate empty space;
- every section reveals on scroll or lifts on hover without adding meaning;
- icons are emojis or mismatched visual families;
- the design looks distinctive in one hero but becomes a stack of identical sections below it.

These are critique prompts, not prohibited styles. Keep a common pattern when it is a deliberate brand or usability choice. Change the one or two choices that most improve clarity and product identity; avoid adding novelty everywhere.

## Choose icons for meaning

Icons must serve user comprehension. Keep an icon when it improves navigation, recognition, or understanding of an action, object, or state. Remove icons that only make a surface look modern or fill empty space. Do not ban a library or familiar symbol when it is useful.

Treat these as signals to question in context, especially when repeated as decorative feature-card art:

- Sparkles for AI-powered everything;
- magic wand for generate or enhance;
- brain or robot for AI;
- rocket for getting started or launch;
- lightning bolt for fast or instant;
- shield check for security;
- trending arrow or growth chart for growth and impact;
- globe for global access;
- workflow nodes for automation;
- target for precision or goals.

Avoid assigning several of these to generic claims such as “Lightning fast,” “Enterprise-grade security,” “Powered by AI,” or “Accessible anywhere” when they do not explain the actual product. Do not give every heading an icon in an identical rounded tile. Prefer an icon tied to the real entity or task, such as users, a queue ticket, a clock, or a display in a clinic queue interface. Use one coherent icon family, stroke weight, and sizing system, and provide accessible names when icons convey meaning. Decorative icons should be hidden from assistive technology.

Familiar AI symbols can genuinely help users recognize AI functionality; their familiarity alone is not a reason to remove them. Ask whether each icon adds product-specific meaning and whether text already communicates the same thing.

## Compose for content

- Vary layout only when content hierarchy, narrative, or task changes. Repeated data should remain consistent and easy to scan.
- Let type size, line length, whitespace, image crops, alignment, and section rhythm carry hierarchy before adding decoration.
- Use art direction or full-bleed imagery when the brand and content benefit from it. Use text-led or quiet layouts when that better serves the brief.
- Keep the primary action visually clear and the supporting actions subordinate.
- Use realistic content at final density. Avoid lorem ipsum and fake customer claims in visual review; mark synthetic data clearly.

## Direction check

Before implementation, confirm that the direction explains:

1. Why this hierarchy serves the user’s task.
2. Which choices come from existing product truth and which are new.
3. What specific detail makes the result recognizable without relying on trend effects.
4. How the same language adapts to small screens and non-success states.

If these answers are not clear, inspect the product more or resolve the smallest material brief gap before polishing.
